Archilochus

A poet and soldier from the Greek island of Paros. Of all his work, only scraps survive, copied by later writers. One scrap is a single line about two animals.

Nobody knows what he meant. One old reading is plain woodland sense: the fox has a hundred tricks, the hedgehog has one, and the one works. The fox sniffs, circles, digs and pounces. The hedgehog rolls into a ball of spines, and the fox goes home hungry.

The shieldHe wrote a poem about throwing his shield in a bush to run from a battle, then said he would buy another one just as good. Greek soldiers were not supposed to say that.
Erasmus, 1500The Dutch scholar put the fox-and-hedgehog line in his Adagia, a big book of old sayings, and it travelled across Europe.
SpinesA European hedgehog carries about 5,000 to 7,000 spines. A muscle along its back pulls them into a ball.
Many tricksRed foxes hunt mice under snow by listening, then leaping high and diving nose first.

Isaiah Berlin sorts the thinkers

In 1953 the Oxford thinker Isaiah Berlin wrote an essay called The Hedgehog and the Fox. He split great writers in two.

Hedgehogs see the world through one big idea that explains everything. Foxes chase many ideas, often loose and even in conflict, and enjoy the world as it comes. Berlin later said he meant it as a game. People have played it ever since.

Philip Tetlock counts the guesses

From 1984 to 2003 the psychologist Philip Tetlock asked 284 experts to forecast world events. Elections, wars, markets: 82,361 forecasts in all. Then he waited to see who was right.

Most experts did little better than a chimp throwing darts. But one group did better: the foxes, who used many small ideas, said “maybe” a lot, and changed their minds. The hedgehogs, sure of one big theory, did worst, and worst of all on their own subject. His book is Expert Political Judgment (2005). Later he ran a forecasting contest with thousands of volunteers, and his best ones, the “superforecasters”, beat professional analysts.

Forest forecasting toy. Each season the forest asks a yes-or-no question: will it snow early, will the beech nuts be thick? The fox says a careful chance. The hedgehog bets big on his one theory. Jim Collins votes hedgehog

In the business book Good to Great (2001), Jim Collins sided with the hedgehog. His “Hedgehog Concept” is where three circles meet:

what you care about most, what you can be the best in the world at, and what drives your economic engine (what earns the money). Find the middle, then do that one thing for years.
